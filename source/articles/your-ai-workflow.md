# Building an AI workflow that lasts

**Covers:** Prompt library, Workflow, Release notes

## Habits beat tips {#overview}
Most people try AI in bursts. They learn a trick, use it for a week and forget it. The people who benefit most build a routine: they know where AI helps in their job, they keep what works and they notice when the tools change. This article shows how to turn scattered experiments into a working habit.

## Workflow {#workflow}
A workflow is the set of steps you follow to get a task done. AI can take part in some of the steps, and not others. A good workflow decides, for each step, who does it: you, the AI, or both with a check.

Take writing a report: you decide the goal and gather the facts, the AI drafts an outline and a first version, you correct it and check every claim, the AI polishes the wording and you approve and send. The AI appears in two places and you remain responsible for the rest.

Write the workflow down once. Include the checks, since they are the steps that people skip when they are busy.

## Prompt library {#prompt-library}
A prompt library is a saved collection of your best prompts and templates, organized so that you can find and reuse them. It can be a document, a notes app or a shared team page. Sort it by task, such as "email replies", "meeting summaries" and "research".

Good entries have a name, what the prompt is for, the template itself, an example of a good result and the date it was last tested. Prompts age, because tools change, so review the library every few months and delete what no longer works. A shared library helps a team: one person's improvement benefits everyone.

## Release notes {#release-notes}
Release notes are short notes that a company publishes about what changed in a new version of its product. For AI tools, they are the best way to keep up, since models and features change often: new abilities, retired models, changed limits and new privacy settings.

You do not need to read everything. Skim the notes for your main tools once a month, looking for three things: a change that improves your workflow, a change that breaks a saved prompt and a change to data or privacy settings.

## A worked example {#example}
Dana, an office manager, builds a routine over a month.

| Week | Action | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lists her recurring tasks: weekly update, meeting notes, supplier emails, expense checks | 4 candidates |
| 2 | Chooses 3 where AI could help; marks the human check for each | A written workflow for each |
| 3 | Saves the best prompt for each in a library, with an example | 3 library entries |
| 4 | Reads the tool's release notes; finds that the model she uses is being retired in 6 weeks | Schedules a retest of her 3 prompts |

Time saved: the weekly update drops from 45 minutes to 15, the meeting notes from 30 minutes to 10 and the supplier emails from 40 to 20. That is 30 + 20 + 20 = 70 minutes a week, about 60 hours a year.

The expense checks stay manual, because a mistake there costs money and she decided that a human should do the whole check.

The release note in week 4 shows why the routine lasts: without it, her prompts would have started giving different results with no warning.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Collecting prompts without ever testing them again. A library full of outdated prompts creates false confidence. Another mistake is automating a step before you understand it by hand. Do it manually a few times first, then decide what to hand over.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A workflow lists the steps of a task and says who does each, including the checks.
- A prompt library stores your best prompts with examples and test dates.
- Release notes tell you when tools change, so skim them monthly.
- Keep the human responsible for steps where mistakes are costly.
