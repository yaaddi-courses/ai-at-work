# Deepfakes, cloned voices and AI-written scams

**Covers:** Deepfake, Voice cloning, Phishing

## Seeing and hearing are no longer proof {#overview}
For most of history, a photo, a video or a familiar voice was good evidence. AI has weakened all three. Realistic fakes now take minutes to make, with free or cheap tools, and scammers use them to impersonate bosses, relatives and officials. So what can you do? The defence is a habit, not a piece of technology: confirm unusual requests in a second way before you act.

## Deepfake {#deepfake}
A deepfake is a video, image or audio clip made by AI that convincingly shows someone saying or doing something that never happened. The word joins "deep learning", the method, and "fake". Early examples had visible flaws, but they are improving fast, and a quick glance can no longer be relied on.

A well-known case: in 2024, an employee of a company in Hong Kong paid out roughly 25 million US dollars after a video call in which the "chief financial officer" and other colleagues were all deepfakes.

There are warning signs, though they are not always present: odd blinking or lip movement, blurry edges around the face or hair, lighting that does not match, unnatural pauses, and a request that is urgent, secret or unusual.

## Voice cloning {#voice-cloning}
Voice cloning uses AI to copy a real person's voice from a short recording. Scammers use it to fake urgent phone calls from family. A few seconds of speech from a social media video or a voicemail greeting can be enough for a rough copy.

The classic scam is a call that sounds like a grandchild or a boss: "I am in trouble, please send money now, and do not tell anyone." The voice may even sound frightened. The defences are easy to remember. Hang up and call back on the number you have saved. Ask a question only the real person could answer. Agree a family safe word. Be wary of any request for gift cards, wire transfers or secrecy.

## Phishing {#phishing}
Phishing is a scam message that pretends to come from someone you trust, to trick you into clicking a link or sharing private details. AI has made it harder to spot. The old signs, such as bad spelling and clumsy grammar, are less reliable now, because AI writes fluent text in any language and can tailor it using facts from your public profiles.

Look at what the message asks for instead: urgency, a link to log in, a change of payment details or secrecy. Check the sender's address, and go to the website yourself instead of using the link.

## A worked example {#example}
You receive a voice message that sounds like your manager: "I am in a meeting and cannot talk. I need you to buy five 100-dollar gift cards for a client. Send me the codes right away." Let's go through it.

1. **Notice the signs:** urgency, secrecy ("cannot talk") and gift cards, which are a common scam payment.
2. **Do not reply to the message.** Do not use the number it came from.
3. **Contact your manager by another route:** call the number you have saved or ask a colleague in person.
4. **Result:** your manager knows nothing about it. The message was a cloned voice.
5. **Report it** to your IT or security team, so others are warned.

Weigh the cost. The check takes two minutes. The gift cards would have cost 5 x 100 = 500 dollars, and the money cannot be recovered once the codes are used.

For a video call with a request for money, use a second channel in the same way, and ask a question that is not found online, such as "what did we order at lunch last Thursday?".

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is believing that you could spot a fake by instinct. Studies show that people are barely better than chance at spotting good fakes. Rely on a process instead of your senses: a second channel, a safe word and a rule that money requests need two approvals.

A second slip is feeling too embarrassed to report an attempt. Reporting helps others.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A deepfake is an AI-made fake video, image or audio that shows someone doing something they did not do.
- Voice cloning copies a voice from a few seconds of audio. Call back on a known number and use a safe word.
- AI-written phishing is fluent, so judge the request, not the spelling.
- Confirm any unusual request by a second channel.
