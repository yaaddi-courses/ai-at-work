# What AI is: from rules to learning to language models

**Covers:** Artificial intelligence, AI, Machine learning, Generative AI, Large language model, LLM, Training data

## A tool you can understand {#overview}
Many people use AI every day without a clear picture of what it is. That is understandable, because the term covers a lot of ground, from the spam filter in your email to the chatbot that writes essays. A rough but accurate picture helps you use these tools well and trust them the right amount. This article builds that picture in five steps, starting from the broadest idea and ending with the thing most people mean today when they say "AI".

## Artificial intelligence {#artificial-intelligence}
Artificial intelligence is computer software that does tasks we usually expect a human mind to do, such as understanding speech, recognizing faces in photos, translating, recommending a film or making a decision. The term was coined in 1956 at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in the United States.

The word "intelligence" can mislead. These systems do not think the way people do, and they have no understanding or intentions. They are tools that are very good at specific kinds of tasks.

## AI {#ai}
AI is the everyday short form of artificial intelligence. Companies often put the label on a product to signal that it uses these methods, so a product that says "AI-powered" may mean anything from a simple rule to an advanced model. When you see the word, ask what the tool actually does.

## Machine learning {#machine-learning}
Machine learning is a way of building AI in which the computer learns patterns from many examples instead of following rules that a person wrote by hand. Compare two ways of building a spam filter. In the first, a programmer writes rules: "if the message contains 'free prize', mark it as spam". In the second, the computer is shown a million emails, each labelled spam or not spam, and works out for itself which features separate them.

The second method copes with situations nobody planned for, because it generalizes from the examples. The price is that it can pick up quirks in the examples, and nobody can easily point to the exact rule it uses. The term machine learning was introduced by Arthur Samuel in 1959.

## Generative AI {#generative-ai}
Generative AI is AI that creates new content, such as text, images, audio or video, instead of only sorting or labelling what already exists. A spam filter sorts. A system that writes an email draft or paints a picture from a description generates.

It does this by learning the patterns in a huge amount of examples and then producing new items with similar patterns. The result looks original, but it is built from what the system learned, which is why questions of copying and ownership come up.

## Training data {#training-data}
Training data is the large collection of examples that an AI learns from before you ever use it: text, images, sound or records. What is in it shapes what the system can do. If the data has few examples of a language, the system will be weak in that language. If it contains mistakes or prejudice, the system will tend to repeat them.

Training happens once, ahead of time, and costs a great deal. After that, the system does not learn from your chat unless the company chooses to include it later.

## Large language model {#large-language-model}
A large language model is a machine-learning system trained on a huge amount of text so that it can read and write human language. "Large" refers to the amount of training text and to the number of adjustable values inside the model, which runs into billions. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are built on models of this kind.

## LLM {#llm}
LLM is the short form of large language model. You will see it in news, product pages and conversations: "this app uses an LLM". It means the same thing.

## A worked example {#example}
Place five everyday tools on the map above:

| Tool | Is it AI? | Machine learning? | Generative? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A calculator | No, it follows fixed rules | No | No |
| An email spam filter | Yes | Yes, trained on labelled emails | No, it sorts |
| A music app suggesting songs | Yes | Yes, learned from listening habits | No, it picks existing songs |
| A photo app that turns a sketch into a painting | Yes | Yes | Yes, it creates a new image |
| A chatbot that drafts a letter | Yes | Yes, using an LLM trained on large amounts of text | Yes |

Notice the nesting: generative AI is a kind of machine learning, and machine learning is a kind of AI. Each tool in the lower rows sits inside the circles above it. That helps you answer a simple question when you meet a new product: what was it trained on, and what does it produce?

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Believing that "AI" is one thing, and that if one AI tool is good at something, all of them are. A system trained to recognize cats says nothing about legal advice. Another mistake is to think the tool learns from you immediately, as a person would. Its learning happened beforehand.

## Key points {#key-points}
- AI is software that performs tasks we expect from a human mind. Machine learning is one way to build it, by learning from examples.
- Generative AI creates new content instead of only sorting existing items.
- Training data shapes what a system can and cannot do.
- A large language model is a machine-learning system trained on huge amounts of text. LLM is the short form.
