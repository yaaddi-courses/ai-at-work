# Privacy, workplace rules and who owns AI output

**Covers:** Personally identifiable information, Confidential information, Data retention, Opt-out, Acceptable use policy, Copyright, Disclosure

## What you type does not stay private by default {#overview}
Typing into a chatbot feels like talking to a private assistant, but your words travel to a company's servers. What happens to them depends on the product, the plan and the settings. At work there is a second layer: your employer's rules, and the law about who owns what you create. This article covers both so that you can use AI without leaking someone's data or breaking a rule.

## Personally identifiable information {#personally-identifiable-information}
Personally identifiable information is any detail that can point to a specific person: a full name, a home address, a phone number, an email address, a national ID number, a date of birth, a medical record or a photo of a face. Several harmless details together can also identify someone: a job title, a small town and an age.

Laws such as the GDPR in Europe and similar rules elsewhere put strict limits on how this data may be handled. As a rule, do not paste it into a public AI tool. Replace it first: "Customer A", "the client's city".

## Confidential information {#confidential-information}
Confidential information is private material that your employer or your clients expect you to keep secret: unreleased plans, contracts, prices, source code, financial results and customer records. Even without a law, leaking it can break your contract.

A tool may keep what you send it, so a confidential document pasted into a public chatbot may be outside your control. In 2023, engineers at a large electronics company pasted internal code into a public chatbot, and the company then restricted its use. Use the approved company tool, if there is one, for work material.

## Data retention {#data-retention}
Data retention is how long a company keeps the data you give it, such as your chat history, and whether it can use that data later. Read the privacy policy for three things: how long chats are stored, who can read them (staff may review some for safety), and whether they are used to train future models.

Business and enterprise plans often promise shorter retention and no training on your data. Free consumer plans often do not.

## Opt-out {#opt-out}
An opt-out is a setting that lets you say no to something, for example letting the company train its AI on your chats. Many tools use training on your data by default and offer an opt-out in the privacy settings. Find the setting and turn it off if you want to keep your chats out of training. Remember that opting out of training does not delete what is already stored.

## Acceptable use policy {#acceptable-use-policy}
An acceptable use policy is a set of rules from your employer or school about what you may and may not do with certain tools. For AI, it often says which tools are approved, what data may be entered, whether output may be used in customer work and when you must tell someone. Read it, and ask if it is unclear. "I did not know" is a weak defence.

## Copyright {#copyright}
Copyright is the legal right of a creator to control who may copy, share or sell their work. It affects AI in two ways. First, the training of models on copyrighted work is the subject of court cases in several countries. Second, the ownership of what an AI produces is unsettled. The US Copyright Office has said that material created entirely by AI, without human authorship, cannot be copyrighted, though a person's own creative choices in selecting and arranging it may be protected.

Practical advice: do not ask AI to copy a living artist's style for commercial use without checking the rules, and do not assume you own the output exclusively.

## Disclosure {#disclosure}
Disclosure is telling people when AI helped create your work. Some workplaces, schools, journals and publishers require it, and some customers care. Even when it is not required, honesty protects your reputation. A short line is usually enough: "Drafted with AI and edited by me."

## A worked example {#example}
You want AI to help with a complaint letter from a customer.

| Step | Action | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Check your acceptable use policy: it allows the approved company tool, not public chatbots, for customer data | Acceptable use policy |
| 2 | Remove the customer's name, address and order number; write "Customer A" | Personally identifiable information |
| 3 | Leave out the unreleased product code name | Confidential information |
| 4 | Confirm the company tool keeps chats for 30 days and does not train on them | Data retention |
| 5 | In a personal account, turn off "improve the model for everyone" | Opt-out |
| 6 | Edit the draft, and add "Drafted with AI" if the policy asks | Disclosure |

Six checks take about three minutes. Skipping step 2 would send a real person's details to an outside company, which may be a legal breach.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Thinking that deleting a chat deletes the data. The company may keep copies for a period set in its policy. Another mistake is assuming that a paid personal plan is as private as a company plan. Read each one.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Do not paste personal or confidential information into tools that your employer has not approved.
- Check the retention policy and use the opt-out for training where available.
- Follow your workplace's acceptable use policy.
- Ownership of AI output is unsettled, so do not assume exclusive copyright.
- Disclose AI help when required, and when honesty calls for it.
